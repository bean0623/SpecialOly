Understanding Behavior
DURING A SESSION

Start with what you see.

Behavior can be communication. Instead of guessing right away, notice what changed, look at the situation, and try one simple response.

START HERE “What happened right before this?”

The same behavior can mean excitement for one athlete, stress for another, or something completely different.

WHAT ARE YOU SEEING?
01 · JUMPING / REPEATED MOVEMENT

Don't assume it means one thing.

Jumping, rocking, pacing, or another repeated movement might be excitement, self-regulation, waiting, stress, or sensory overload. Look at the rest of the situation.

It might be excitement.

Maybe their turn is coming, they just made a basket, or they're about to do something they really enjoy.

It might help them regulate.

Repeated movement can sometimes help an athlete manage energy, waiting, or sensory input.

It might be overload.

Look for other clues: covering ears, trying to leave, looking distressed, or having trouble responding.

Try
  • Stay calm.
  • Use fewer words.
  • Tell them what's happening next.
  • Give them some space.
  • Reduce noise if they seem overwhelmed.
Avoid
  • Immediately calling it “bad behavior.”
  • Yelling “stop.”
  • Repeating directions rapidly.
  • Assuming they are ignoring you.
02 · OVERWHELMED

Make the situation smaller.

A loud gym, whistles, crowds, unexpected changes, lots of instructions, or being surrounded by people can become too much.

Things you might notice

  • Covering ears
  • Trying to leave
  • Repeated movement becoming more intense
  • Looking tense or distressed
  • Suddenly not responding
  • Crying, yelling, or shutting down
Try
  • Use a calm voice.
  • Reduce talking.
  • Give them physical space.
  • Move toward a quieter area if appropriate.
  • Offer one simple choice.
  • Give them time.
Avoid
  • Getting louder.
  • Crowding around them.
  • Forcing eye contact.
  • Rapid-fire questions.
  • Unexpected touching or grabbing.
Try: “Break or stay?” or “Want somewhere quieter?”
03 · WALKING AWAY

Don't turn it into a battle.

Walking away might mean the athlete needs a break, doesn't understand what is happening, feels uncomfortable, or simply wants some space.

Try this

  • Stay nearby if it is safe.
  • Walk calmly with them instead of immediately demanding they return.
  • Give them a little time.
  • Reconnect using something clear and positive.
“Basketball is next. Want to come see?”

If you're unsure whether the athlete needs physical guidance, ask first or check with someone who knows them well.

04 · NOT RESPONDING

Pause before repeating yourself.

Not responding doesn't automatically mean the athlete is ignoring you. They may need more processing time, may not understand the instruction, or may communicate in another way.

Try this order

1. Get their attention.

Say their name naturally.

2. Give one short instruction.

Keep the language simple.

3. Wait.

Give them time to process and respond.

4. Show what you mean.

Point, gesture, or demonstrate.

5. Offer a choice if it helps.

For example, “Walk or run?”

Instead of repeating: “Go stand over there.”

Point to the spot and say: “Stand here.”
05 · FRUSTRATED

Look for what's making things harder.

The activity might be confusing, difficult, tiring, uncomfortable, too loud, or simply not what they want right now.

Try

  • Use fewer words.
  • Demonstrate instead of explaining again.
  • Offer a short break.
  • Give two simple choices.
  • Notice their face, gestures, and body language.
  • Encourage without adding pressure.
“Want help?”   or   “Break or one more?”
06 · SAYING NO

Notice the message first.

“No” isn't always spoken. It might look like shaking their head, pushing something away, turning away, using a hand gesture, selecting something on AAC, or another clear signal.

Start by acknowledging it.

“Okay. We can wait a minute.”

You can encourage the athlete to try again later without acting like their first response never happened.

If you're unsure

Ask a coach, caregiver, or someone who knows the athlete: “Is that how they usually tell us no?”

Safety comes first.

If an athlete is at immediate risk of getting hurt, leaving a safe area, or hurting someone else, get the coach or event staff involved right away. This guide is for communication support, not emergency response.

Every athlete is different. These examples are starting points, not a way to diagnose what a behavior “means.”
