Understanding Behavior
In the moment

Start with what you see.

Something feels off? Don't rush to label it. Take a look at what's happening, give the athlete a beat, and figure out what might help.

↕
What's going on?
Notice first. Then respond.
Start here

What happened right before this?

That's usually a better first question than “How do I make this stop?” The same behavior can mean excitement, stress, waiting, confusion, sensory regulation — or something completely different.

What are you seeing?
Jumping / repeated movement

Don't assume it means one thing.

Jumping, rocking, pacing, or repeating the same movement can happen for a bunch of reasons. The context matters way more than the movement by itself.

Ask yourself What's happening around them right now?
They might just be excited.

Their turn is coming, they made a basket, they saw someone they like, or they're doing their favorite activity. If they're happy and safe, you may not need to stop it at all.

The movement might be helping them regulate.

Moving can sometimes help with waiting, energy, sensory input, or just getting settled.

Or things might be getting to be too much.

Check for other clues: covering their ears, trying to leave, looking tense, or suddenly having a hard time responding.

Try

  • Stay relaxed.
  • Give them some room.
  • Tell them what's coming next.
  • Say less if they seem overloaded.
  • Let safe movement happen when it doesn't need fixing.

Skip

  • “Stop that!” right away.
  • Assuming they're ignoring you.
  • Piling on directions.
  • Treating every repeated movement as a problem.
Overwhelmed

Make the moment smaller.

A loud gym, whistles, people moving everywhere, a sudden schedule change, or too many instructions at once can turn a normal session into a lot to handle.

♪ ! •••

You might notice:

Covering ears or moving away from noise
Looking tense, upset, or suddenly very quiet
Repeated movement getting more intense
Having a much harder time responding

Try

  • Say less for a minute.
  • Keep your voice calm.
  • Give them some space.
  • Move somewhere quieter if that makes sense.
  • Offer one simple choice.
  • Give the athlete time to settle.

Skip

  • Getting louder.
  • Crowding around them.
  • Rapid-fire questions.
  • Forcing eye contact.
  • Unexpected grabbing or touching.
Something simple works. “Want a break?”
“Stay here or somewhere quieter?”
Walking away

Don't make it a power struggle right away.

Maybe they need a break. Maybe the activity doesn't make sense yet. Maybe they just want a little space. You don't have to solve that in the first two seconds.

→

Try

  • Stay nearby if it's safe.
  • Keep your tone easy.
  • Give them a moment.
  • Reconnect with something concrete.

Skip

  • Repeating “come back” ten times.
  • Blocking them in unless safety requires it.
  • Turning the moment into a standoff.
Try reconnecting, not commanding. “Basketball's next. Want to come see?”
Not responding

Give them a beat.

No immediate answer doesn't automatically mean “they aren't listening.” They might be processing, figuring out what you mean, getting ready to move, or communicating in another way.

1. Get their attention.

Say their name naturally.

2. Keep the cue short.

One idea at a time.

3. Wait.

Give them a real chance to respond.

4. Show it.

Point, gesture, or demonstrate if the words aren't landing.

Instead of adding more words: Point to the spot and say, “Stand here.”
Frustrated

Something may be harder than it needs to be.

The drill might be confusing. They might be tired. The room might be loud. Or maybe they just don't want to do this exact thing right now.

Try

  • Use fewer words.
  • Show the drill.
  • Offer help.
  • Give a short break.
  • Offer two simple options.

Watch for

  • Tight eyebrows.
  • Pulling away.
  • A sudden expression change.
  • More intense movement.
Keep it easy. “Want help?”
“Break or one more?”
Saying no

Notice the message first.

“No” might be a word. It might also be a head shake, a hand pushing something away, turning their body, a gesture, or a choice on AAC.

✋

You can still encourage the athlete later. But start by showing them that their communication actually means something.

Acknowledge it. “Okay. We can give it a minute.”
Not sure whether a gesture means “no” for that athlete? Ask someone who knows them: “Is that how they usually tell us no?”
One exception: safety.

If an athlete is about to get hurt, leave a safe area, or someone else is in immediate danger, get the coach or event staff involved. Safety comes before figuring out the perfect communication strategy.

These are starting points, not a dictionary for what every behavior means. The athlete in front of you is always the best source of information.
