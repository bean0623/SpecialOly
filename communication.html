Non-speaking athletes

Less speech doesn't mean less to say.

An athlete might communicate with a look, a gesture, a point, movement, a device, a sound, or a mix of all of them. Your job isn't to make them communicate your way. It's to figure out how the two of you can connect.

Look for the response.
1
Talk to them directly.

Use their name. Keep them in the conversation. Don't automatically turn to the person beside them for every answer.

2
Say it clearly, then give it a beat.

Don't rush to repeat yourself. Give the athlete a real chance to process and answer.

3
If the words aren't landing, show it.

Point. Gesture. Demonstrate the drill. Pick up the ball and show what happens next.

4
Pay attention to the small stuff.

Turning away, reaching, pointing, facial expressions, movement, or a hand gesture may all be part of the conversation.

5
Make “yes” and “no” easy.

A nod, head shake, point, thumbs-up, gesture, speech, or AAC can all count.

6
Don't jump in and answer for them.

A pause can feel long when you're waiting. That's okay. Give them the space to finish.

One more thing: When you can, spend time with the same athlete across sessions. Familiar faces make communication way easier.
AAC & communication

Communication doesn't have to sound like speech.

Some athletes use AAC. Others mix speech with gestures, pointing, pictures, writing, or facial expressions. Whatever works for the athlete is the point.

Do

  • Wait for them to finish.
  • Keep talking to the athlete.
  • Respond to the message.
  • Let their communication system stay available.

Skip

  • Finishing every message for them.
  • Talking only to their aide.
  • Turning everything into a test.
  • Moving their device out of reach.
Think conversation, not quiz. If the athlete communicates “water,” respond to “water.” You don't need to turn the moment into “Okay, now can you say water?”
Useful phrases

Keep it natural. Just make it clear.

You don't need to talk like a robot. Shorter language just makes it easier to catch the important part.

When you're giving them time

“Take your time.” “I'll wait.” “Show me.” “Can you show me again?”

When you're offering a choice

“Water or break?” “Again or all done?” “Walk or run?” “Want some help?”

When something is about to change

“One more, then break.” “Basketball next.” “Two minutes, then we switch.” “First this. Then water.”
If repeating yourself isn't helping, don't just keep repeating. Say less. Show more. Point to what you mean.
During sports

Keep the cue quick.

In the middle of a drill, the athlete usually needs the next useful piece of information — not a whole speech.

→
Cue + show.
Keep it moving. If the words aren't working, show one pass or point to the spot.
“Ready?” “Shoot or pass?” “My turn. Your turn.” “More shots or water?”
Waiting can be tough when nobody knows when their turn is coming. Give a clear cue before the start.
“Ready. Set. Go.” “Walk or run?” “Your turn is next.” “Rest or one more?”
Pair a short cue with pointing or a quick demonstration.
“Kick or pass?” “Ready?” “Again?” “Stop or go?”
What families want you to know

The people who know the athlete can save you a lot of guessing.

Parents, caregivers, coaches — and of course the athlete themselves — may already know exactly what helps. Asking isn't a sign that you don't know what you're doing. It's usually the smart move.

One parent shared

A few things can make a really big difference:

  • Give a heads-up before things change. If you know you're switching activities soon, let the athlete know what's coming.
  • Your face matters. Some athletes watch facial expressions really closely. If you look annoyed or upset, they may think you're upset with them. Smile, stay warm, and don't be afraid to make the encouragement obvious.
  • Give a little space and watch their face. Tight eyebrows, pulling away, or a sudden change in expression can be a sign to slow down and check in.
  • If they clearly say “no,” notice it. Sometimes giving them a minute works a lot better than pushing harder.
  • Encourage them. A little positive energy goes a long way.
One of the best questions you can ask: “What usually works for them?”
Quick respect check

Treat the athlete like an athlete.

Simple doesn't mean childish. Helpful doesn't mean taking over.

Talk to them, not around them.

If the athlete is right there, start with them.

Keep the language age-appropriate.

Short and clear is great. Baby talk isn't.

Slower beats louder.

If something isn't landing, volume usually isn't the missing piece.

Ask before physically helping.

Don't assume a hand on someone's arm, shoulder, or back is automatically helpful.

It's okay to say you didn't understand.

“Can you show me again?” is way better than pretending.

Give them time to finish.

Don't answer for someone just because the pause feels awkward.

Good communication doesn't mean doing everything perfectly. Be curious, be patient, and pay attention to what works for the athlete in front of you.
