When words aren't enough

Look for the answer, not just the words.

A response might be a look, point, sound, movement, facial expression, gesture, or AAC. Less speech does not mean less to communicate.

1
Talk to the athlete directly.

Use their name. Don't automatically turn to the person beside them for the answer.

2
Say one thing, then wait.

Give them a real chance to process. A 5–10 second pause can be useful.

3
If words aren't landing, show it.

Point, gesture, demonstrate the drill, or show the next step.

4
Notice how they answer.

A nod, head shake, reaching, turning away, pointing, or facial expression can all mean something.

Don't jump in and answer for them. A pause can feel long when you're waiting. Give them the space to finish.
AAC

What is AAC?

AAC means Augmentative and Alternative Communication. It can add to speech or replace speech when speech is not the easiest way to communicate.

Think conversation, not quiz. If the athlete communicates “water,” respond to “water.” You don't need to make them prove it again.
Useful phrases

Keep it natural. Just make it clear.

You don't need to sound robotic. Shorter language simply makes the important part easier to catch.

When you're giving them time

“Take your time.” “I'll wait.” “Show me.” “Can you show me again?”

When you're offering a choice

“Water or break?” “Again or all done?” “Walk or run?” “Want some help?”

When something is about to change

“One more, then basketball.” “Your turn is next.” “Two minutes, then we switch.” “First this. Then water.”
If repeating yourself isn't helping, don't just keep repeating. Say less. Show more. Point to what you mean.
During sports

Give the next useful cue — not a speech.

In the middle of a drill, keep the instruction short. Then give the athlete time to respond or show what you mean.

What families want you to know

The people who know the athlete can save you a lot of guessing.

Parents, caregivers, coaches — and the athlete themselves — may already know exactly what helps.

One parent shared
Give a heads-up before things change. Let them know what's coming next.
Your face matters. Some athletes watch facial expressions closely. Smile, stay warm, and make encouragement obvious.
Give a little space and watch their face. Tight eyebrows, pulling away, or a sudden change can mean slow down and check in.
If they clearly communicate “no,” notice it. Giving them a minute can work better than pushing harder.
Encourage them. Positive energy can make a real difference.
One of the best questions you can ask: “What usually works for them?”
Quick respect check

Helpful without taking over.

Simple communication should still feel respectful, age-appropriate, and two-way.

Talk to them, not around them. If the athlete is there, start with the athlete.
Keep language age-appropriate. Short and clear is useful. Baby talk isn't.
Slower beats louder. If something isn't landing, volume usually isn't the missing piece.
Ask before physically helping. Don't assume touching an arm, shoulder, or back is automatically helpful.
Don't grab their communication or mobility tools. AAC, communication boards, wheelchairs, and walkers stay with the athlete.
It's okay to say you didn't understand. “Can you show me again?” is better than pretending — then give them time to finish.
