COMMUNICATION

Find a way to connect.

Speech is only one way people communicate. Watch, listen, show, gesture, and give the athlete time to respond.

WHAT DO YOU NEED?
NON-SPEAKING ATHLETES

Don't mistake less speech for less understanding.

Not speaking doesn't tell you exactly what an athlete understands. Keep talking to them directly, but use the whole interaction — words, gestures, demonstrations, facial expressions, and time.

01
Say it clearly. Then wait.

Give the athlete a moment before repeating yourself. Silence doesn't always mean they didn't understand.

02
Show what you mean.

Demonstrate the drill, point to the spot, or show the equipment instead of relying only on words.

03
Watch how they respond.

Facial expression, gestures, turning away, reaching, pointing, or repeated movement may all be communication.

04
Make yes and no easy.

Give the athlete a simple way to answer: nodding, shaking their head, pointing, thumbs up, gestures, speech, or AAC.

05
Don't rush to speak for them.

Give them the chance to answer for themselves, even when it takes a little longer.

06
Build familiarity.

When possible, working with the same athlete across sessions can make communication and trust easier over time.

07
Get to know the person.

Ask real questions. What sport do they like? What music do they enjoy? What makes them laugh? Communication gets easier when there's a relationship behind it.

AAC & COMMUNICATION

Communication doesn't have to look like speech.

An athlete may use one method or several at the same time. Pay attention to what works for them.

💬 Speech
👋 Gestures
☝ Pointing
🙂 Facial expression
✎ Writing / pictures
▣ AAC device

If an athlete uses AAC

  • Give them time to finish.
  • Keep speaking directly to the athlete.
  • Respond to what they're communicating.
  • Don't make them repeat something just to prove they can.
  • Don't take away or move their communication system unless they ask.
Think conversation, not quiz.
If an athlete communicates “water,” respond to the message. You don't need to turn it into “Can you say water?”
USEFUL LANGUAGE

Short usually works better.

You don't have to sound robotic. Keep your tone natural — just make the message easy to follow.

Giving time

“Take your time.” “I'll wait.” “Show me.” “Can you show me again?”

Offering choices

“Water or rest?” “Again or all done?” “Walk or run?” “Do you want help?”

Transitions

“One more, then break.” “Basketball next.” “Two minutes, then we switch.” “First this. Then water.”
Instead of adding more words, add information.
Point. Demonstrate. Show the object. Tell them what's coming next.
SPORTS

Keep cues quick.

During a drill, athletes usually need the next useful cue, not a long explanation.

“Ready?”

“Shoot or pass?”

“My turn. Your turn.”

“More shots or water?”

If the cue isn't working, demonstrate one pass or point to the spot rather than repeating a long instruction.
“Ready. Set. Go.”

“Walk or run?”

“Stop.”

“Rest or one more?”

If the athlete is waiting, tell them when their turn is coming.
“Kick or pass?”

“Ready?”

“Again?”

“Stop or go?”

Pair short cues with pointing or a quick demonstration.
FROM FAMILIES

Small things can make a big difference.

Families and coaches often know an athlete's communication style really well. Ask what works instead of trying to figure everything out on your own.

One parent shared:
  • Give some warning before changing activities. Knowing what's coming next can make transitions easier.
  • Facial expressions can matter a lot. Keep your expression warm and show encouragement clearly.
  • Give the athlete a little physical space. Watch their face and body language for signs that they may be uncomfortable.
  • If the athlete clearly signals “no,” acknowledge it. Sometimes giving them a few minutes works better than pushing right away.
  • Give time, encouragement, and positive feedback.
A useful question:
“What usually helps?”
QUICK RESPECT CHECK

Treat the athlete like an athlete.

Speak directly to them.

Don't automatically direct everything to the person beside them.

Use age-appropriate language.

Simple language doesn't have to sound childish.

Speak slower, not louder.

Volume doesn't make an instruction easier to understand.

Ask before physically helping.

Don't assume grabbing, guiding, or touching will help.

Don't pretend you understood.

It's okay to say, “Can you show me again?”

Give them time.

Don't answer for them just because the pause feels long.

Communication is personal. Use these ideas as starting points, then pay attention to the athlete in front of you.
