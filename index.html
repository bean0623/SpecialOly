A quick guide for communicating with athletes clearly, respectfully, and confidently during every session.

Before your session

Five things to remember.

You don't need to memorize a manual. Start with these.

1 of 5

See the athlete first.

Speak directly to the athlete. Say their name. Don't talk only to their parent, coach, or aide.

“Hi Alex. Ready for basketball?”
2 of 5

Keep it simple.

Use short phrases and give one instruction at a time. If something isn't landing, make it clearer instead of just saying more.

“First, line up.”
“Next, basketball.”
“Then, water.”
3 of 5

Show, don't only tell.

Point, gesture, demonstrate the drill, or show the equipment. Sometimes seeing the action makes the instruction much easier to understand.

Say “Pass” while showing one pass.
4 of 5

Give them time.

Ask the question, then wait. Some athletes need more time to process, move, point, speak, or use a device.

Ask → wait → watch → respond.
5 of 5

Give real choices.

Choices make communication easier and give athletes more control over what happens next.

“Water or rest?”
“Again or all done?”
“Walk or run?”
During a session

What are you seeing?

Don't jump straight to “they aren't listening.” Start with what is happening and think about what the athlete may be communicating.

Jumping or repeated movement

Start with one question: What happened right before this? The same movement can mean very different things for different athletes.

It could be excitement.

Maybe their turn is coming, they just did something well, or they're excited about the next activity. If they're safe and happy, you may not need to stop the movement at all.

It could be waiting or uncertainty.

If they've been standing around for a while, give them something concrete: “You're next,” or “Two more people, then your turn.”

It could be sensory overload or self-regulation.

Look at the rest of their body language. Are they covering their ears? Trying to leave? Looking distressed? Is the gym especially loud or crowded?

What to try

  • Stay calm.
  • Use fewer words.
  • Tell them what's happening next.
  • Give them some space.
  • Move somewhere quieter if that seems helpful.

If an athlete seems overwhelmed

The goal isn't to force them back into the activity as quickly as possible. Help the situation feel manageable again.

Try this

  • Reduce noise and talking if you can.
  • Give them some physical space.
  • Use a calm voice and short phrases.
  • Offer simple choices: “Break or stay?” “Water?” “Help?”
  • Give them time before asking another question.
  • Ask a coach, parent, or experienced volunteer what usually helps.
Avoid adding more pressure. Don't yell, rapidly repeat directions, force eye contact, or unexpectedly grab or touch the athlete.

If an athlete walks away

Don't immediately turn it into a confrontation. If it's safe, stay nearby and walk with them for a moment.

Once things feel calmer, reconnect with something concrete and positive:

“Basketball is next. Want to come see?”

Give them a reason to reconnect rather than simply repeating “come back” again and again.

If physical guidance may be needed, check with the athlete or someone who knows them first rather than assuming touch will help.

If the athlete isn't responding

They may need more time, may not understand the instruction, may be focused on something else, or may communicate differently.

Try this order

  • Say their name and give one short instruction.
  • Wait.
  • Show or point to what you mean.
  • If needed, repeat or rephrase once.
  • Offer a simple choice.
Instead of repeating “Go stand over there,” point to the spot and say, “Stand here.”

If the athlete seems frustrated

Look for the reason before trying to fix the behavior. The activity may be confusing, tiring, uncomfortable, too loud, or simply something they don't want right now.

Try

  • Use fewer words.
  • Show the activity instead of explaining it again.
  • Offer a short break.
  • Give two simple choices.
  • Notice gestures, facial expressions, and body language.

If the athlete is telling you “no”

“No” may come through speech, a hand gesture, turning away, shaking their head, an AAC device, or another clear signal.

Acknowledge it first.

“Okay. We can wait a minute.”

You can encourage participation later without pretending their first response didn't happen.

Core protocol

When an athlete communicates without speech.

Not speaking doesn't tell you exactly what someone understands. Talk to the athlete directly, pay attention to how they respond, and learn the communication style that works for them.

01

Say it clearly. Then give it a second.

Don't rush to repeat yourself. Give the athlete time to process. If it doesn't land, try saying it another way or showing what you mean.

02

Show the activity.

Demonstrate the drill once before asking them to try. Pointing, gestures, and real objects can make instructions much easier to follow.

03

Watch their body language.

A push-away gesture, turning away, repeated movement, facial expression, or hesitation may be telling you something. Don't assume you know what it means. Look at the situation around it.

04

Don't turn walking away into a battle.

If it's safe, stay nearby. Give them a little time, then reconnect using something clear and positive about what is happening next.

05

Use gestures naturally.

Point to the basketball. Mimic drinking when offering water. Nod yes. Shake your head no. Let your body support what you're saying.

06

Make yes and no easy.

Give the athlete a clear way to answer. That might be nodding, shaking their head, pointing, thumbs up or down, a gesture, speech, or a device.

07

Build familiarity.

When possible, stay with the same athlete across sessions. New people can take time to get comfortable with.

08

Get to know them.

Ask simple, real questions. What did they eat for breakfast? What sport do they like? What makes them laugh? Communication gets easier when there's an actual relationship.

09

Bring some energy.

Enthusiasm can make an activity feel inviting. Be encouraging without overwhelming the athlete.

Communication & AAC

Speech is only one way to communicate.

An athlete may communicate through speech, movement, gestures, pictures, writing, facial expressions, pointing, or a device. Pay attention to all of it.

If an athlete uses AAC Give them time to finish. Don't speak for them. Respond to what they're communicating rather than testing them. Keep their communication system available to them.

Useful phrases

“Show me.” “Take your time.” “I'll wait.” “Do you want help?” “Stop or keep going?” “Your choice.” “Can you show me again?”
During sports

Keep cues short.

Tap a sport for a few phrases you can use without overloading the athlete.

“Shoot or pass?”

“My turn. Your turn.”

“More shots or water?”

“Ready?”

Pair the words with a quick demonstration whenever it helps.
“Walk or run?”

“Ready. Set. Go.”

“Stop.”

“Rest or one more?”
“Kick or pass?”

“Again?”

“Goal?”

“Stop or go?”
From families

The people who know the athlete can teach you a lot.

Parents, caregivers, coaches, and the athlete themselves may already know what helps communication go smoothly.

One parent shared a few things that make a big difference:

  • Give advance warning before a transition or new activity.
  • Facial expressions matter. Stay relaxed, smile naturally, and show encouragement.
  • Give the athlete some space and watch their own facial expressions and body language.
  • If they clearly signal “no,” acknowledge it. Giving them a few minutes may work better than pushing immediately.
  • Give time, encouragement, and positive feedback.
Ask when you're unsure. “What usually helps?” can be one of the best questions a volunteer asks.
Respect

Treat the athlete like an athlete.

Speak directly to them.

Use age-appropriate language.

Speak slower, not louder.

Ask before helping physically.

Don't pretend you understood. Ask again.

Don't answer for them.

Give them time.

This guide is designed as a quick communication resource for volunteers. Every athlete is different. When you're unsure, ask the athlete, coach, caregiver, or an experienced volunteer what works best.
